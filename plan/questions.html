Every question, and why it is worded that way

Greg's condition: framing is 90 percent of the result, so name the bias instead of claiming there is none. Each question below carries its note. Print this page to PDF if you want to mark it up.

Study one: the name

One name per person, drawn at random from F1R3FLY, F1R3, FIRE, KAVRIX, F1R3LY. Seeing one name and being tested on it is how a real first encounter works; showing the names side by side turns the study into a beauty contest, and preference votes favour the plainest name every time (Catchword, NameStormers, SurveyMonkey all say the same).

Before the name: What best describes your work? / How old are you?

Sorts people into the groups on the Who page. Bands, not exact ages, so people answer.

The name, alone, for five seconds. Plain text, no logo, no colour.

No logo, because the logo carries the brand work already done and would test that instead. All caps, because that is how the wordmark reads. Five seconds is the standard first-impression exposure.

1. In one or two words, what do you think this company does?

The most useful question, asked first, before any hint. The answer we fear is "crypto." The answers we want are near "software," "infrastructure," "AI."

Then one line, identical for every name: "It makes infrastructure software for AI systems and enterprises."

Naming firms give product context before perception questions; without it people misread a bare name. One line, same word count in every cell, so no name gets a richer story.

2. Say the name out loud, then type how you said it, spelled the way it sounds.

Catches "F one R three fly," "fire fly," "fee-ree." Typing is a stand-in for a voice recording, which would kill completion.

3. How easy was it to know how to say it? 1 to 5
4. Would you be comfortable saying this name out loud in a work meeting? Yes / Yes, but I'd feel a little silly / No

The middle option matters. Leetspeak's real risk is not confusion, it is embarrassment. This is the only question that measures that.

5. If you heard this name spoken and had to type it into a browser, how confident are you that you'd spell it right? 1 to 5
6. Listen to this clip once, then type the company name you heard.

The hard test, and the one that decides the F1R3FLY-versus-F1R3 argument. A voice says "firefly" (or "fire," "fire," "kavrix," "firely"). Does hearing it produce F1R3FLY, FIREFLY, or Firefly? FIRE, plain, is the only name that can score 100 percent here; that is why it is in the set: it shows the cost of the leetspeak as a number. Pilot clips are a computer voice; the paid wave gets one human voice, same pace, for all five.

7. Which of these words fit the name? Pick up to three. Serious / Playful / Technical / Trustworthy / Gimmicky / Modern / Dated / Young / Corporate / Confusing / Memorable / Forgettable

Six warm, six cool, shuffled for every person. "Gimmicky" and "Confusing" are the honest failure modes for leetspeak; leave them out and we are rigging it.

8. Does the name remind you of any other company, product, or thing? (optional)

Where the Amazon Firefly trademark concern Nicholas raised lives, plus the TV show, the Adobe product, and Firefly Aerospace.

9. Had you seen or heard this name before today? Yes / No / Not sure

People who already know the brand are read separately. For the name study this has to come after the name, or asking it would be the exposure.

10. Anything else about the name? (optional)
Email for one question tomorrow (optional): "Yesterday you saw a company name. Type it as best you remember." Then: how sure are you, 1 to 5.

Real recall, the thing a brand is for. Expect a low return; even 20 answers per name is informative. Scored three ways: exact, phonetic (FIREFLY for F1R3FLY), and gone. Naming firms do this unannounced; we tell people a second part exists but not what it asks.

Study two: the website

Two parts. Part one is the standard first-impression test: a picture of the f1r3fly.io home page, exactly as it fits a phone or a laptop screen, shown for ten seconds, then questions. Ten rather than five because a dense infrastructure page is a complex stimulus, and purpose recognition is the measure that improves between five and ten seconds (Smashing Magazine's 2023 study). Part two is the live site for up to two minutes, then comprehension questions.

Before anything: work, age, and "Before today, had you heard of a company called F1R3FLY?"

Awareness is asked first here because the page shows the name anyway. Brand-aware people are read as their own group.

Part one, after ten seconds: What was this page about? / In your own words, what do you think this company does? / Who do you think this is designed for? / What action did the page want you to take? / How trustworthy did the page feel? 1 to 5

Lyssna's and Koji's standard wordings, open questions first. Note what the home page shows in ten seconds today: the logo, "a subsidiary of F1R3FLY Industries," and a scroll arrow. If the answers come back "no idea," that is a finding about the fold, not a failure of the test.

Part two, after the live site: Now that you've looked around, what does this company do? / What do they offer? / Which industry is this company in? (twelve options, shuffled; "Cryptocurrency or blockchain" is one of them) / Which of these words describe the company? pick up to three (eight warm, eight cool) / How well did the site explain what the company does? 1 to 5 / Which of these would you do next, if anything? / Was anything unclear or confusing?

"Crypto" never appears in a question, only as one option in a balanced list, after the open answers. Asked the other way round, people repeat the word back (Pew's open-versus-closed finding: 58 percent pick a listed option that only 35 percent volunteer unprompted). Asking "what does this company do" twice, at ten seconds and after two minutes, shows what the rest of the site adds to the fold.

Six rules the design follows

  1. Open questions before closed ones. Lists put words in mouths.
  2. No loaded words in a question. "Crypto," "blockchain," "hype" live only in balanced option lists.
  3. One idea per question. Anything with "and" in it gets split.
  4. Options shuffled for every person; "Not sure" and "Other" pinned last.
  5. No agree/disagree statements. People agree with statements; offer choices instead.
  6. Nobody sees the name or the page twice, and nobody is told what the questions will be.

Sources of bias, named

  • Sample. Hannah's lists skew Gen X and know her. Nicholas's skew enterprise UK. Greg's skew academic and Gen Z. Hence the channel tags.
  • One name per person hides preference. Deliberate. Preference is what Greg and Hannah already disagree about; this measures what the name does.
  • FIRE is a stacked deck on spelling. It wins question 6 by construction. It is a weak candidate on trademark and search, which this study does not measure.
  • Hannah wrote the questions and has a position. Mitigation: Greg and Nicholas review; the pilot catches leading wording; a research professional reads it before the paid wave.
  • Computer voice in the pilot. Replaced by one human voice for the paid wave.
